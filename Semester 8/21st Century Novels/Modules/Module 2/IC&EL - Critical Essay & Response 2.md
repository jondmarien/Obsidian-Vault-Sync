# Part A – Discussion

## Subject: Unlocking Silence: Parallel Quests in Extremely Loud & Incredibly Close
In Jonathan Safran Foer’s Extremely Loud & Incredibly Close, the profound national trauma of September 11th is distilled into the deeply personal quest of nine-year-old Oskar Schell. Reeling from the death of his father in the World Trade Center, Oskar discovers a mysterious key in a vase, setting him on a frantic journey across New York City to find the lock it opens. This quest is not merely a search for an object, but a search for meaning, connection, and a way to articulate an unspeakable loss. However, Oskar’s journey does not happen in a vacuum. It runs parallel to the silent, sixty-year quest of his grandfather, Thomas Schell Sr., a man so shattered by the trauma of the Dresden firebombing that he has lost the ability to speak. By examining Oskar’s loud, external search for answers alongside his grandfather’s mute retreat from his own past, Foer explores the varied and often fraught ways individuals attempt to communicate trauma. While Oskar seeks to give voice to his pain through action and inquiry, and his grandfather seeks refuge in silence, both characters are ultimately searching for a way to bridge the gap between their internal suffering and the outside world, a journey that leads them not to simple answers, but to the profound discovery that healing begins with the vulnerable act of sharing one's story.

Oskar’s quest is a direct, if chaotic, attempt to "work through" his grief and guilt. He is searching for a tangible link to his father, believing the key holds a final message or puzzle, a continuation of the "Reconnaissance Expeditions" they once shared. The search provides purpose and structure in a world that has become terrifyingly random. But on a deeper level, Oskar is searching for a way to manage the overwhelming guilt he feels over his father's last moments. He secretly carries the burden of the final phone calls from the North Tower, confessing much later in the novel, "He needed me, and I couldn’t pick up. I just couldn’t pick up. I just couldn’t. Are you there? He asked eleven times" (Foer, qtd. in SparkNotes ). This secret isolates him, creating a chasm between his inner reality and his outward behavior. He tells his therapist, "My insides don't match up with my outsides" (Foer 108 . Oskar’s communication is a whirlwind of invention, letter-writing, and incessant talking, all defense mechanisms to externalize a pain he cannot process internally . His scrapbook, titled *Stuff That Happened to Me*, is a key visual element Foer uses to represent this effort. It is an archive of images—some found, some taken by him—that allows Oskar to curate his trauma, to "put oneself into a certain relation to the world that feels like knowledge – and therefore, like power" (Sontag, qtd. in Lezana 11). Through these textual and visual artifacts, Oskar tries to build a coherent narrative from the fragments of his shattered world.

In stark contrast, Oskar’s grandfather, Thomas Schell Sr., embodies the "acting out" of trauma, where suffering is not processed but perpetually and silently re-lived. Haunted by the bombing of Dresden, which claimed his first love, Anna, and their unborn child, he literally loses his voice. According to one scholarly analysis, for trauma victims in the novel, "language is no longer an effective tool for expressing trauma; as a result, the victims project their traumas through their bodies" (Bouacida et al. 1240). His mutism is the physical manifestation of his psychic wounds. He communicates through notebooks filled with single words or phrases and the tattoos of "YES" and "NO" on his hands, a system that prevents any true emotional expression. He lives as a "renter" in his own family's life, a ghost haunted by his past. His quest is not an outward search, but an internal stasis, a lifelong attempt to avoid the pain of memory. He confesses in a letter his profound regret over this self-imposed silence: "maybe if I'd said, 'I'm so afraid of losing something I love that I refuse to love anything,' maybe that would have made the impossible possible. Maybe, but I couldn't do it, I had buried too much too deeply inside me" (Foer 215). His failure to communicate his trauma has not protected him; it has only guaranteed his isolation, costing him his relationship with his wife and son.

Both Oskar and his grandfather initially fail in their attempts to communicate their trauma because they are unable to share the core of their pain. Oskar hides the answering machine, and his grandfather hides his entire past. Their intersecting paths highlight this shared struggle, encapsulated in the grandfather's heartbreaking admission: "We had everything to say to each other, but no ways to say it" (Foer 286) . Foer’s use of unconventional visual elements—photographs, blank pages, and scribbled-over text—mirrors this breakdown of communication, reflecting, as one analysis notes, "the functioning of a traumatized mind" . The photographs and drawings interspersed throughout the text are not mere illustrations; they are integral to the characters' fractured attempts to express the inexpressible, showing how trauma disrupts linear narrative.

Ultimately, neither character finds what they initially set out for, but they find something more meaningful. Oskar's quest does not lead to a message from his father; the key belongs to a man named William Black and opens a safe-deposit box containing nothing of value to Oskar. The "quest, not the finding," is what matters (Course Hero) . The true "unlocking" happens when Oskar finally confesses his secret about the phone calls to William Black, the final person on his list . In that moment of shared vulnerability, he is no longer alone with his guilt. As one scholar notes, this confession allows him to be a child again, to be cared for instead of feeling the impossible burden of protecting everyone. His grandfather, moved by Oskar's journey, takes a monumental step out of his silence. He joins Oskar in a painful, cathartic act: digging up the empty coffin of the son he barely knew. It is a shared, non-verbal acknowledgment of their grief. The novel’s final visual element, a flip-book created from photographs of a man falling from the World Trade Center, presented in reverse, shows Oskar's ultimate lesson. He cannot change the past, but he can reframe his narrative—not as one of falling, but of returning, of piecing life back together. In the end, both characters learn that communication is not about finding perfect words but about the willingness to be heard, seen, and understood, even in the absence of language.

Your analysis of the "door left open" metaphor really struck me—it's such a powerful way to conceptualize Oskar's unresolved grief. What I find particularly compelling about your reading is how it connects to the novel's broader exploration of incomplete narratives. That open door isn't just about Oskar's personal loss; it represents all the stories that remain unfinished after trauma strikes.

Your point about the grandfather's silence functioning as its own form of communication made me reconsider my own analysis. While I focused on the contrast between Oskar's loud quest and his grandfather's muteness, you've highlighted something crucial: silence can be just as expressive as words. The grandfather's "YES" and "NO" tattoos aren't just communication tools—they're literally inscribed trauma, permanently marking his body with the minimal language he can manage. This connects beautifully to your Codde citation about language's limitations.

I'm also intrigued by your observation about Foer blurring the lines between victims, perpetrators, and witnesses. This seems to challenge readers to move beyond simple categorizations of suffering, which might explain why the novel's visual elements—those blank pages and fragmented images—force us to actively participate in constructing meaning rather than passively consuming it. Your analysis suggests that Foer isn't just representing trauma; he's creating a space for collective witnessing and understanding.
